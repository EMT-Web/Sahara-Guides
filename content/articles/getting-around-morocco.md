Morocco's transport works on two systems. In the **north-west**, a modern railway connects the big cities, including Africa's first high-speed line. **Everywhere else**, from the Atlas to the Sahara, the road is the only way, by bus, shared taxi, rental car or private driver. Most itineraries combine both.

## Which transport for which journey?

| Journey | Best option | Alternatives |
|---|---|---|
| Tangier ↔ Rabat ↔ Casablanca | Al Boraq high-speed train | Motorway |
| Casablanca ↔ Marrakech | Train (about 2 h 40) | Motorway (about 3 h), CTM bus |
| Casablanca/Rabat ↔ Fes | Train (about 3–4 h) | Motorway |
| Fes ↔ Marrakech | Train (about 7 h) or private driver | Flight via Casablanca rarely saves time |
| Fes ↔ Chefchaouen | CTM bus or driver (3.5–4 h) | Grand taxi |
| Marrakech ↔ Essaouira | Supratours or CTM bus (about 3 h) | Driver, rental car |
| Marrakech ↔ Sahara (Merzouga) | Private driver or tour (break the journey) | Overnight bus |
| Anywhere ↔ Errachidia, Ouarzazate, Dakhla | Domestic flight | Long-distance bus |

## Trains (ONCF)

The national railway, **ONCF**, runs the network. Trains are comfortable, air-conditioned and inexpensive by European standards.

### Al Boraq high-speed train

**Al Boraq** opened in 2018 and runs between **Tangier, Kenitra, Rabat and Casablanca**. The dedicated high-speed track runs from Tangier to Kenitra, with trains continuing on upgraded conventional lines to Rabat and Casablanca. The full Tangier–Casablanca journey takes about **2 hours 10 minutes**.

An extension of the high-speed line to **Marrakech** is under construction. It is not yet open; the authorities have indicated completion around the end of the decade, so do not plan around it.

### Conventional trains

Conventional services link Casablanca with **Marrakech** (about 2 hours 40 minutes), **Fes** (about 3.5–4 hours, via Rabat and Meknes) and **Oujda** in the east, as well as the airport line from Casablanca Mohammed V airport. Fes to Marrakech takes about 7 hours on a direct train.

### Tickets and classes

- Buy tickets on the **ONCF website or app**, or at the station. On Al Boraq and at busy times, book ahead; seats are assigned.
- **First class** gives more space and an assigned seat on most services, for a modest supplement. On long journeys it is worth it.
- Take food and water on long trips; trolley service is limited on some trains.

> [!NOTE]
> The railway does not reach Chefchaouen, Essaouira, Agadir, the Atlas Mountains or the Sahara. ONCF's partner bus company, Supratours, connects some stations to these destinations with combined tickets.

## Long-distance buses

Two companies run reliable, comfortable intercity coaches:

- **CTM**: the largest network, covering almost every town of any size, including Chefchaouen, Essaouira, Agadir, Ouarzazate and the south.
- **Supratours**: linked to ONCF, often timed to connect with trains, with routes to Essaouira, Agadir, the south and the desert.

Book on the companies' websites or at their own bus stations (which are often separate from the main *gare routière*). There is usually a small fee for luggage in the hold. Cheaper local buses also exist, but they are slower and less comfortable.

## Taxis

- **Petit taxis** are small city taxis, colour-coded by city (beige in Marrakech, red in Fes and Casablanca, blue in Rabat). They carry up to three passengers within city limits. Ask for the meter ("*le compteur*"); if the driver refuses, agree the fare first or take another taxi. Fares rise at night.
- **Grand taxis** are larger cars, usually older Mercedes or newer people-carriers, that run fixed routes between towns from a taxi rank. They leave when full (six passengers), and you can pay for empty seats to leave sooner or to have more space. You can also hire a grand taxi for a day trip.
- **Ride-hailing apps** operate in some Moroccan cities, though availability and legal status vary. Ask your hotel what works locally.

## Renting a car and driving yourself

Driving is the most flexible way to explore the south, and main roads are paved all the way to Merzouga and M'Hamid.

**Rules and realities**

- Drive on the **right**. Seat belts are compulsory, and using a phone while driving is prohibited.
- Typical speed limits are lower than many visitors expect in towns and villages, and **police speed checks are frequent**, including on rural roads. Watch the signs carefully.
- Expect **police and gendarmerie checkpoints**, especially at town entrances. Slow down, and carry your licence, passport and rental documents.
- **Avoid driving at night.** Pedestrians, cyclists, animals and vehicles without lights are common on rural roads.
- **Mountain roads** such as the Tizi n'Tichka are well surfaced but winding, with heavy trucks and occasional winter closures.
- **Never drive a rental car into dunes or on desert tracks.** Use a 4x4 with an experienced driver.
- Fuel stations are frequent on main routes but sparse in the far south; fill up when you can.

**Paperwork:** you need a valid driving licence and, depending on your nationality and the company, an International Driving Permit. Check the rental agreement for insurance excess, and photograph the car on collection.

## Hiring a private driver

For many itineraries, especially routes over the Atlas to the desert, a private driver is the best compromise between flexibility and ease. Drivers handle navigation, parking, checkpoints and long distances, and they often know good places to stop.

When booking, ask:

- Is the company **licensed for tourist transport**?
- What is included: fuel, tolls, the driver's food and accommodation, transfers to the desert camp?
- What vehicle, and does it have working air conditioning and seat belts in every seat?
- What is the maximum driving time per day? Good companies will push back on unrealistic plans.

## Domestic flights

**Royal Air Maroc** operates most domestic routes, many of them via its Casablanca hub. In recent years **Ryanair** and **Air Arabia** have added point-to-point domestic routes, which come and go by season. Useful routes for travellers include flights to **Errachidia** (for Merzouga), **Ouarzazate** and **Zagora** (for the south) and **Dakhla**.

Flying rarely saves time between the big northern cities, which the train links well, but it can save a full day of driving to the south. Always confirm routes and schedules directly with the airline; small airports may have only a few flights a week.

## Ferries to and from Spain

Ferries cross the Strait of Gibraltar between **Tarifa and Tangier** (Tangier Ville port, close to the city) and between **Algeciras and Tanger Med** (a large port about 45 km east of Tangier). Crossings take from about an hour. Expect heavy traffic and long queues in July and August, when many Moroccans living in Europe travel home.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming you can take the train everywhere.** Plan road transport for the mountains, the desert, Chefchaouen and Essaouira.
- **Underestimating mountain driving.** A 200 km mountain road can take four hours.
- **Booking a rental car for the city.** In Marrakech and Fes, a car is a burden. Pick it up when you leave town.
- **Leaving tickets to the last minute** on Al Boraq or at Eid and in August, when trains and buses sell out.

## The bottom line

Take the train in the north-west, use CTM or Supratours for direct connections to towns off the railway, and hire a driver or rent a car for the south. Build realistic travel days, and the journey between places becomes one of the best parts of the trip.
