The honest answer is that **Morocco is a safe country to visit for the vast majority of travellers**, and it is one of the most visited countries in Africa. Violent crime against tourists is uncommon. What most people remember, good and bad, is the intensity of the medinas: the noise, the persistence of some sellers, and the occasional attempt to overcharge. Knowing what to expect turns that from stressful into simply part of the experience.

This guide is updated whenever the main government advisories change. Each claim about official advice links to the source, and the date at the top of the page shows when we last checked them.

## What the official travel advisories say

Governments set their own advisory levels, and they rarely match exactly. Here is how three of the main English-speaking governments described Morocco when we last checked. Australian travellers should read Smartraveller, linked in the sources below.

| Government | Overall level | Main reason | Regional exceptions |
|---|---|---|---|
| United States | Level 2: Exercise increased caution | Terrorism | Read the advisory for areas with increased risk |
| Canada | Exercise a high degree of caution | Threat of terrorism | Avoid all travel within 20 km of the Algerian border and near the Berm in Western Sahara; avoid non-essential travel to Western Sahara |
| United Kingdom | Does not advise against travel to Morocco as a whole | Terrorism | Listed on the FCDO's separate "Regional risks" page |

**What this means in practice.** Level 2 is the same category the US gives to many popular European destinations. The terrorism warnings reflect a general threat rather than a specific one; Moroccan security services are active and have disrupted plots, including one reported in July 2026. Sensible precautions are the same as in any major city: stay aware in crowded places, follow the instructions of local authorities, and keep an eye on the news during your trip.

**The regional exceptions do not affect normal tourist routes.** Marrakech, Fes, Chefchaouen, the coast, the High Atlas, the Dades and Todra gorges, Merzouga and Erg Chigaga are all outside the areas covered by the stricter advice.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> Advisories change. Read the current version from your own government before you book and again before you travel, and check that your travel insurance is valid for your destination. Insurers may refuse claims for areas your government advises against.

## The risks you are most likely to meet

### Touts, hustlers and unofficial guides

This is the issue almost every visitor encounters, especially in Marrakech, Fes and Tangier. Typical approaches include:

- **"That way is closed."** Someone tells you the street, the square or the museum is closed and offers to show you another way, which ends at a shop or a request for money. Check for yourself; it is rarely true.
- **The free tour of the tanneries.** A friendly local leads you to a leather shop terrace "for free", then expects a payment and a purchase. It is perfectly fine to visit the tanneries; just agree any fee first or go with an official guide.
- **Unofficial guides.** In the Fes medina especially, young men may attach themselves to you and ask for payment at the end. A polite, firm "*la, shukran*" ("no, thank you"), repeated, and continuing to walk, is usually enough.
- **Henna and photographs on Jemaa el-Fna.** Henna artists may start drawing on your hand before agreeing a price, and performers expect payment for photos. Agree any price before anything starts.

None of this is dangerous. It is mainly annoying, and it is much less intense if you book a licensed guide for your first half-day in each medina. Official guides carry an identity badge, and your riad can arrange one.

### Petty theft

Pickpocketing happens in crowded places: the souks, Jemaa el-Fna in the evening, train stations and buses. Carry a cross-body bag, keep your phone in a front pocket, and leave your passport in the hotel safe unless you need it (carry a photocopy). Snatch theft from motorbikes occurs occasionally in cities; keep your phone away from the kerb.

### Overcharging

Prices in the souks are negotiable, and starting prices for visitors can be several times what a local would pay. Bargaining is normal and good-humoured. Decide what something is worth to you before you ask the price. For taxis, insist on the meter for *petit taxis* in cities, or agree the fare before you get in.

## The real danger: the roads

Road traffic accidents are a far greater risk to visitors than crime. Morocco's main motorways are good, but mountain passes such as the Tizi n'Tichka are winding, trucks and buses overtake on blind bends, and rural roads are shared with pedestrians, donkeys and scooters without lights.

- **Avoid driving at night**, especially in the mountains.
- **Wear a seat belt** in every seat; it is required by law.
- **Choose an experienced driver** if you hire one, and do not be afraid to ask them to slow down.
- **Do not attempt to drive into the dunes** in a hire car. Sand driving needs a 4x4 and experience.

## Advice for women travellers

Many women travel alone or in pairs in Morocco and have excellent trips. The most common problem is verbal attention, comments and persistent conversation in busy areas, which is tiring rather than threatening. What helps:

- **Dress conservatively**, especially outside resort areas: shoulders and knees covered, loose clothing. This is about reducing attention rather than any legal requirement.
- **Walk with purpose** and avoid engaging with persistent approaches. Sunglasses and headphones (with the volume low) help.
- **Choose accommodation with good reviews from women travellers**, and ask your riad to arrange transfers on arrival at night.
- **Join a small group or book a female guide** for medina walks if you prefer. Several cities now have female guides.
- **If you feel unsafe**, step into a shop, café or hotel and ask for help. People will usually help if someone is genuinely harassing you.

## LGBTQ+ travellers

Same-sex sexual relations are illegal in Morocco under the penal code, and public displays of affection are frowned upon for all couples. In practice, LGBTQ+ visitors do travel to Morocco, especially to Marrakech, but discretion is important. Booking a room with two beds avoids questions in some accommodation; most international hotels and many riads are relaxed about this.

## Health and environmental risks

- **Stomach upsets** are the most common health complaint. Drink bottled or filtered water, wash hands before eating, and favour busy food stalls where food is cooked fresh in front of you.
- **Heat and sun** in summer, particularly in the desert. Drink more than you think you need and cover your head.
- **Altitude and cold** in the High Atlas. Winter conditions on Toubkal require mountaineering equipment and an experienced guide.
- **Earthquakes.** The September 2023 Al Haouz earthquake caused severe damage in the High Atlas south of Marrakech. Rebuilding has continued since; the villages most visitors pass through are open, and visiting them supports local recovery. Ask your guide about the current situation in specific villages.
- **Rabies** exists in Morocco. Do not touch stray dogs or cats, or the macaques used by street performers, and seek medical advice immediately if you are bitten or scratched.

## Laws and customs that visitors should know

- **Drugs.** Cannabis is grown in the Rif, but buying or using it for recreation is illegal and penalties can be severe. You may be offered it in the north; decline.
- **Photography.** Do not photograph police, military, government buildings or border areas. Always ask before photographing people, and accept a no.
- **Religious sites.** Most mosques are closed to non-Muslims. The Hassan II Mosque in Casablanca is the best-known exception and can be visited on a guided tour.
- **Ramadan.** Avoid eating, drinking and smoking in public during daylight hours, out of respect.
- **Criticising the monarchy or religion** in public can be a criminal offence. Avoid political discussions with strangers.

## Emergency numbers

| Service | Number |
|---|---|
| Police (in towns and cities) | 19 |
| Royal Gendarmerie (rural areas and highways) | 177 |
| Ambulance and fire | 15 |

Large cities also have a tourist police presence around the main sights. Save your country's embassy or consulate contact details on your phone; the US, UK and Canada all have embassies in Rabat, and the US also has a consulate general in Casablanca.

## A simple safety checklist

1. Read your government's current advisory and register your trip if your country offers it.
2. Buy travel insurance that covers medical evacuation and the activities you plan.
3. Book your first night's accommodation and an airport transfer in advance.
4. Book an official guide for your first visit to each medina.
5. Carry a copy of your passport; leave the original locked away.
6. Avoid night driving and choose experienced drivers.
7. Drink bottled or filtered water and protect yourself from the sun.

## The bottom line

Morocco rewards a little preparation. Treat touts with polite firmness, keep valuables out of sight, take road safety seriously, and respect local customs. Do those things and the overwhelming likelihood is that you will remember the hospitality, not the hassle.
