Moroccan food reflects the country's history: Amazigh (Berber) traditions of slow cooking and grains, Arab spices and sweet-savoury combinations, Andalusian refinement brought by refugees from Spain, and French and Mediterranean influences in the cities. Above all, it is home cooking. The best meals are often in family kitchens, riads and small local restaurants rather than in elaborate "palace" dining rooms.

## The essentials

### Tagine

A tagine is a stew slow-cooked in a shallow clay dish with a tall conical lid that returns condensation to the food. Classic combinations include:

- **Chicken with preserved lemon and olives**, perhaps the most famous.
- **Lamb or beef with prunes and almonds**, sweet and rich, often served at celebrations.
- **Kefta** (spiced meatballs) in tomato sauce, with eggs cracked on top.
- **Vegetable tagine** with seasonal vegetables.

Tagines are eaten from the dish with bread. The best are cooked slowly over charcoal; be wary of restaurants serving dozens of identical tagines at speed.

### Couscous

Steamed semolina grains, traditionally rolled by hand, served in a mound with meat and seven vegetables, or with caramelised onions and raisins (*tfaya*). In Moroccan homes, couscous is the **Friday lunch**, eaten after midday prayers. Many restaurants serve the best version only on Fridays.

### Pastilla (bastilla)

A speciality of Fes: thin warqa pastry layered with pigeon or chicken, eggs, almonds, cinnamon and saffron, baked and dusted with icing sugar and cinnamon. The sweet-savoury combination surprises many visitors. Seafood pastilla is a popular modern variation.

### Tanjia

The signature dish of **Marrakech**: beef or lamb with preserved lemon, garlic, cumin and saffron, sealed in a clay urn and cooked slowly for hours in the ashes of a hammam furnace. Traditionally a bachelors' dish, prepared by men for a day out.

### Mechoui

Lamb slow-roasted until it falls apart, eaten with cumin and salt. In Marrakech, a lane near Jemaa el-Fna is famous for it at lunchtime.

### Harira

A tomato, lentil and chickpea soup with herbs and a little meat, thickened with flour and egg. It is the traditional dish for breaking the fast during **Ramadan**, served with dates and sweet pastries, and is eaten year-round as a cheap, filling meal.

## Breakfast and breads

Bread (*khobz*) accompanies almost every meal and is treated with respect: it is never thrown away carelessly.

- **Msemen**: square, flaky griddle pancakes, eaten with honey or butter.
- **Baghrir**: "thousand-hole" semolina pancakes, soaked with melted butter and honey.
- **Harcha**: a crumbly semolina griddle bread.
- **Amlou**: a dip of argan oil, almonds and honey from the south-west. Excellent on bread.
- **Bissara**: a thick fava bean or split pea soup with olive oil and cumin, a winter breakfast from street stalls, especially in Fes and the north.

A typical riad breakfast includes bread, pancakes, olive oil, honey, jam, eggs, fresh orange juice and mint tea or coffee.

## Salads and small dishes

Moroccan "salads" are often cooked vegetable dishes served as a spread of small plates:

- **Zaalouk**: smoky aubergine and tomato with garlic and cumin.
- **Taktouka**: grilled peppers and tomato.
- **Carrots with cumin and orange blossom water**, beetroot with cumin, and lentils.
- **Moroccan salad**: diced tomato, cucumber and onion.

## Regional specialities

| Region | Try | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Marrakech | Tanjia, mechoui | Look for small specialist restaurants near the souks |
| Fes | Pastilla, bissara, khlii | Khlii is preserved spiced meat, often cooked with eggs |
| Essaouira and the coast | Grilled fish and seafood | Choose from the stalls at the port and have it grilled |
| The south and Merzouga | Madfouna | "Berber pizza": flatbread stuffed with spiced meat and onions, baked in sand or a clay oven |
| The Atlas | Rfissa, tagines with walnuts | Hearty mountain cooking; rfissa is shredded msemen with chicken and lentils |
| The Souss | Amlou, argan oil | Argan trees grow only in south-western Morocco |

## Street food

- **Grilled brochettes** of lamb, beef, chicken or liver.
- **Snail soup** (*babbouche*) in a spiced broth, sold from carts in the evenings.
- **Sfenj**: Moroccan doughnuts, fried to order in the morning.
- **Sandwiches** of grilled kefta, sausage or fried fish with salad and spicy sauce.
- **Fresh orange and sugarcane juice.**

Eat where locals queue. A busy stall turns food over quickly, which is the best indicator of freshness.

## Sweets

- **Chebakia**: flower-shaped pastries fried and dipped in honey with sesame, especially during Ramadan.
- **Kaab el ghazal** ("gazelle horns"): crescent pastries filled with almond paste and orange blossom water.
- **Briouates**: small fried pastry triangles with almond paste and honey (or savoury fillings).
- **Sellou**: a rich mix of toasted flour, almonds and sesame, eaten during Ramadan and after childbirth.

## Mint tea

Mint tea (*atay*) is the centrepiece of Moroccan hospitality. Chinese gunpowder green tea is brewed with fresh mint and usually a lot of sugar, then poured from height to create a froth. You will be offered tea in homes, shops and riads; accepting at least one glass is polite. If you prefer less sugar, ask for it "*bla sukkar*" or "*shwiya sukkar*" (a little sugar) when it is being made.

## Eating etiquette

- **Wash your hands** before eating; a basin and jug are often brought to the table.
- **Eat with your right hand**, using bread to scoop food from your side of the shared dish.
- **Wait for the host** to begin and to offer more.
- **Bread is respected**: do not waste it or put it on the floor.
- **During Ramadan**, avoid eating and drinking in public in daylight.
- **Saying "*bismillah*"** before eating and "*al-hamdulillah*" afterwards is customary for Muslims; guests are not expected to, but it is appreciated.

## Vegetarian, vegan and other diets

Vegetarians eat well in Morocco: vegetable tagines and couscous, salads, lentils, bissara, breads, eggs and fruit are everywhere. Vegans should ask about **smen** (aged butter) and stock. Useful phrases: "*bla lham*" (without meat) and "*makanakoulch lham*" (I do not eat meat). Gluten-free travellers will find bread and couscous everywhere, but grilled meats, tagines and salads are naturally gluten-free; communicate clearly and carry a translated card.

Most meat in Morocco is **halal**. Pork is rarely available outside a few international hotels and shops.

## Food safety

- Drink **bottled or filtered water**, and use it for brushing your teeth if you have a sensitive stomach.
- Choose food that is **cooked fresh and served hot**.
- Peel fruit, or wash it in bottled water.
- Be cautious with salads and ice in very basic places in the first days of your trip.
- Carry rehydration salts in case of a stomach upset.

## Where to eat well

- **Riads** often cook the best home-style food; book dinner a day ahead so the cook can shop.
- **Small local restaurants** near the souks for tagines, tanjia and grilled meats at lunch.
- **Food tours** in Marrakech and Fes introduce street food with someone who knows which stalls are good.
- **Cooking classes** in riads and cooking schools usually include a market visit, and are one of the best ways to understand the cuisine.
